# On Punishment Kubbs

A post by Chris Hodges of Des Moines Kubb, dated April 22, 2011, from the Des Moines Kubb
blog, in Kubb On's archive. It gives the defender three places to put a punishment kubb
and when he uses each, under the U.S. National Kubb Championship rulebook (version 1.1,
linked), whose definition it quotes. It is his own practice; it counts nothing. Its one
set of figures, angles of the window for a king shot, he calls his estimate.

| `p1.2` | Chris Hodges | punish | Questions for the defender: behind the king, on the baseline or behind a centre-line pin? Are you winning or behind, how many field kubbs does the opponent have to throw, and how skilled are they, and will a king shot's pressure get to them? | said |
| `p1.3` | (rulebook) | rules | The rulebook he quotes: each kubb gets one fault (coming to rest where it cannot be raised in bounds); a second fault makes it a punishment kubb, which the defender may place anywhere in their half, no closer than one baton length to the king or any field marking pin. | said |
| `p1.4` | Chris Hodges | punish | Three places he uses: the baseline, the sideline and behind the king. | said |
| `p1.5` | Chris Hodges | punish | The baseline is probably the most common placement, conservative and about as safe as it gets. | said |
| `p1.6` | Chris Hodges | punish | He puts it on the baseline when behind by two or more base kubbs, to win one back, or when he has left the opponent an advantage line, so as not to make it easier for them. | said |
| `p1.7` | Chris Hodges | punish | When the game is about even he is more aggressive: the sideline farthest from the other field kubbs, to improve his odds of an advantage line. | said |
| `p1.8` | Chris Hodges | punish | His rule of thumb on the sideline: about half a metre in from the baseline for every field kubb the attacking team threw. Two thrown, one of them the punishment kubb: about 1 m in. Six thrown: only about 1 m off the centre line. | said |
| `p1.9` | Chris Hodges | punish | With more than six thrown he will probably not place it within a metre of the centre line; he goes for the kill and forces a king shot instead. | said |
| `p1.10` | Chris Hodges | punish | A baton length behind the king is the most aggressive placement. He generally does it only when up by two or more base kubbs, or when the opponent is throwing a lot of field kubbs. | said |
| `p1.11` | Chris Hodges | punish | Even then it depends on the other field kubbs: if they lie in a tight cluster he may be more conservative. | said |
| `p1.12` | Chris Hodges | punish | It also depends on the opponent: is their short game shaky or are they a shooter, and will the pressure cost them a baton or two? | said |
| `p1.13` | Chris Hodges | king | Behind the king he creates a 5 m shot that most players make several times a game; what changes is the pressure, since a miss to the wrong side can lose them the game. | said |
| `p1.14` | Chris Hodges | punish | An instant win is a bonus; if the opponent misses once or twice from caution the placement still worked, since at the stage of forcing a king shot a couple of misses matter. | said |
| `p1.15` | Chris Hodges | punish | Handedness: he used to centre it a baton length straight behind the king. Now he moves it one kubb width to the thrower's off side, to the left for a right-hander and the right for a left-hander. | said |
| `p1.16` | Chris Hodges | punish | His reason: the rule that both feet stay inside the sidelines tends to put the thrower's legs in the way of a shot at a kubb on their off side. | said |
| `p1.17` | Chris Hodges | punish | His estimate of the margin for error: moving it one kubb width shrinks the good-side window from 0.6 to 0.2 degrees and opens the weak side only from 0.4 to 0.6 degrees. The total margin stays similar, but the throw is less natural. No basis given beyond his estimate. | said |
| `p1.18` | Chris Hodges | punish | Wherever it goes, and especially on the baseline or sideline, avoid creating a double or worse. | said |
| `p1.19` | Chris Hodges | punish | Face the kubb towards where you expect the throw to come from: a flat face shows a smaller cross section and makes a smaller target. | said |
| `p1.20` | Chris Hodges | punish | The other school points an edge at the thrower, for more lateral support and so a direct hit needed to topple it. He has seen many kubbs jostled without falling and noticed no difference by orientation, so he sides with the smaller target. No count given. | said |
| `p1.21` | Chris Hodges | levels | No fixed logic works in every situation; strategy is as much instinct as analysis. | said |
