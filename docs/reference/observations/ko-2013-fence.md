# Fence Building 101 – (or #occupybatons!)

A post by Chris Hodges of Des Moines Kubb, dated March 7, 2013, from the Des Moines Kubb
blog, in Kubb On's archive. It is advice to the defender on raising thrown field kubbs
so that they cost the attackers as many batons as possible. It gives no figures and no
data; it is one experienced player's method. It follows a post from the day before on
footprints and raising rules, which it assumes the reader has read. It credits "Dano"
for the point about the ground and names Team Knockerheads in a photo caption. Three
small diagrams show four field kubbs in an X.

| `p1.1` | Chris Hodges | raise | Most of kubb's tactics come in two phases: the defender choosing how to stand thrown field kubbs, and the attacker choosing how to take down the groups that result. | said |
| `p1.2` | Chris Hodges | raise | First, stand the forced kubbs: leaners, kubbs half in bounds, and those that must go a certain way so that others can be raised in bounds. With no choice in them, do them first and then look at the rest. | said |
| `p1.3` | Chris Hodges | raise | Next, the obvious ones: runners and exact: "'east-west'" kubbs (neither term defined), that can be raised away from the pile to soak up an extra baton. | said |
| `p1.4` | Chris Hodges | raise | Then reassess: estimate how many batons the kubbs now standing will need. His club calls that number par, from golf. | said |
| `p1.5` | Chris Hodges | raise | Judge the group from where the attackers stand, in both senses: how would you attack it, and how would kubbs and baton move after impact? Facing, the way each kubb is turned, matters most. He likens the judgment to combination shots in billiards, made harder by square corners. | said |
| `p1.6` | Chris Hodges | raise | Repeat that estimate baton by baton through the attack. The further ahead, the harder to predict, but guessing beats not guessing. | said |
| `p1.7` | Chris Hodges | raise | Look for holes in the pattern to stand a kubb in. More space is not always better: it can give flying kubbs room to do damage, while standing closer to another kubb can take it out of the line of fire or leave other kubbs isolated. | said |
| `p1.8` | Chris Hodges | raise | The inside double: four field kubbs lying in an X. Standing each away from the centre gives the most space but makes two possible doubles, which a good shooter may clear with two batons. | said |
| `p1.9` | Chris Hodges | raise | Standing two of the four towards the centre makes an easy double in the middle but isolates the two outer kubbs, which most likely soaks up an extra baton. | said |
| `p1.10` | (diagrams) | raise | Three diagrams: four kubbs lying in an X; the same with all four raised outward, crossed out in red; and with two raised towards the centre and two outward, circled in green. | shown |
| `p1.11` | Chris Hodges | raise | Space across the pitch matters much more than space along it: a kubb is easy to push up the pitch and hard to push across it. He usually prefers a fairly tight fence across the pitch to a looser line along it. | said |
| `p1.12` | Chris Hodges | raise | When every way of raising a kubb leaves a double, make sure the line through the double points away from other field kubbs. | said |
| `p1.13` | Chris Hodges | raise | If that does not decide it, choose the double that lies most across the pitch: a shot across can be a metre or two longer, and it is less likely to send debris downrange. | said |
| `p1.14` | Chris Hodges | surface | Every pitch has bumps, divots and clumps; a leaning kubb can fall to a touch that a firmly standing one would survive. | said |
| `p1.15` | Chris Hodges | raise | Location matters more than footing: better to take a whole baton, even with a glancing hit, than to stand firm and risk a double. When direction does not change the baton count, stand the kubb so the attacker must hit it squarely. | said |
| `p1.16` | Chris Hodges | raise | Go through every remaining choice before standing any doubtful kubb, since a later choice may undo an earlier one; decide where all of them go first. | said |
| `p1.17` | Chris Hodges | raise | The whole team should talk through the likely outcomes, but one player should raise the kubbs in the main group (a teammate may handle obvious outliers). Once a kubb is up it is committed; one pair of hands avoids miscommunication. Giving away even one kubb through poor placement is too many. | said |
| `p1.18` | Chris Hodges | raise | Raising is playing the percentages: a group judged at three batons and cleared with one may just be lucky bounces. If it keeps happening, rethink how you judge par. | said |
| `p1.19` | Chris Hodges | baseline | Good raising over time, with the odd miss by the opponent, protects your base kubbs and lets you reach the advantage line. | said |
